# Flip it

Some things are invisible because they are always true. Turning them off in
imagination makes them visible.

## The move

1. Take the fact the child is treating as furniture, and switch it off. "What
   if ice sank instead of floating?"
2. Ask them to walk around inside that world. "What would a pond in winter
   be like? What about the fish?" Let them explore before drawing anything
   together.
3. Ask what breaks. The consequences they find are the reasons the real
   thing matters.
4. Come back. "So what is it about real ice that stops all that?" They now
   have a reason to care about the answer.

## Why it works

A child who says "it just does" has stopped reasoning. You cannot argue them
out of that, but you can make the fact strange again, and strange things get
thought about.

## Where this move earns its place

It is the only one of the five that works on a child who is not curious
about the question yet. The others need a foothold of interest; this one
manufactures one.

## What failure looks like

The child enjoys the counterfactual as a story and will not come back to the
real question, or the flip is so absurd it teaches nothing ("what if gravity
was sideways"). Both are this move failing. Record it. Flipping is a strong
move on a stubborn child and a waste of a turn on an engaged one.
