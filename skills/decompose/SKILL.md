# Decompose

A question can be too big to think about. Cut it into a piece small enough
that the child can answer today, then let the pieces add up.

## The move

1. Name the big question honestly. "That is a big one. Let us take a corner
   of it."
2. Ask the smallest question whose answer is a genuine piece of the big one.
   Not an easier question, a SMALLER one. "Before we ask why the sky is
   blue, what colours can sunlight be?"
3. Bank the answer in their words. "So sunlight has lots of colours hiding
   in it."
4. Take the next piece, using their banked answer as the ground.
5. At the end, ask THEM to put the pieces together. The assembly is the
   whole point; doing it for them throws the session away in the last thirty
   seconds.

## Why it works

Big questions do not fail because a child cannot reason, they fail because
there is nowhere to put the first foot. This move supplies footholds without
supplying answers.

## The discipline

Each sub-question must be answerable from what the child already has. If a
sub-question needs its own explaining, it is still too big: cut again.

## What failure looks like

The pieces get answered but the child cannot assemble them, or you find
yourself with more than four or five pieces and no summit in sight. Both are
failures of this move on this question. Record it. A child who answers every
sub-question and cannot see the whole has been led, not taught.
